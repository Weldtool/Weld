\section{Background and Related Work}
% =====================================================================

Software engineering is being reorganised around assistants that do not merely
complete code but participate in the work \cite{hoda2026agentic,ahmed2025aise}.
As that happens, the design question shifts from how well a model generates code
to how much of the surrounding work an agent should be admitted into, and on
what terms of trust \cite{roychoudhury2025trust}. This study takes the narrowest
useful slice of that question --- how much of a shared workspace an agent may see
and act on --- and measures its effect on the person working alongside it. Four
literatures bear on it, and each leaves the same gap.

\subsection{AI assistants in software engineering practice}

The Copilot-era studies established what developers actually do with an
assistant, and the answer was never ``accept its output.'' Vaithilingam et
al.\ found that a code-generating assistant did not reliably improve task
completion time or success rate, yet most participants preferred it, valuing it
as a starting point while struggling to understand and repair what it produced
\cite{vaithilingam2022expectation}. Barke et al.\ characterised the interaction
as two distinct modes --- \emph{acceleration}, where the assistant supplies a next
step the developer already had in mind and validation is quick, and
\emph{exploration}, where the developer is unsure and uses the assistant to think,
evaluating far more carefully \cite{barke2023grounded}. Mozannar et al.\ made the
hidden cost explicit by instrumenting and labelling the states developers move
through, showing that verifying and reasoning about suggestions consumes a
substantial share of the interaction rather than being a rounding error
\cite{mozannar2024reading}. At survey scale, Liang et al.\ found developers adopt
these tools chiefly to move faster and avoid recalling syntax, while their
dominant complaint is an inability to steer output toward requirements they hold
in their heads \cite{liang2024largescale}.

Across all four, the unit of study is code authored in an editor. Almost none
examines an assistant operating on the coordination surfaces --- issue trackers,
team chat, code review --- where a large share of software work is actually
transacted, and where an agent's usefulness depends on reaching across
boundaries rather than on generating a good token sequence.

\subsection{Context and agent capability}

A parallel literature establishes that context breadth improves what models
produce. Zhang et al.\ showed that iteratively retrieving repository-level
context substantially outperforms completion conditioned on the current file
alone \cite{zhang2023repocoder}. Ding et al.\ built a benchmark deliberately
constructed so that the correct completion \emph{cannot} be inferred from
in-file context, and found that supplying cross-file context lifts performance
sharply \cite{ding2023crosscodeeval}. Jimenez et al.\ pushed the unit of work up
to whole GitHub issues requiring repository-scale reasoning, where the models of
the day resolved only a small percentage of tasks \cite{jimenez2024swebench}.

These are results about model output, measured against ground truth with no
person present. That more context yields better generations does not establish
that it yields a faster, more accurate, or better-calibrated \emph{human}. The
step from benchmark gain to user benefit is exactly where an assistant's value
is realised or lost, and it is not something a benchmark can settle.

\subsection{Fragmentation, context switching, and coordination cost}

The reason workspace scope is worth manipulating at all is that developer work
is fragmented by default. Meyer et al.\ found developers' own accounts of a
productive day centre on uninterrupted progress on a single task, with task
switching experienced as the enemy of it \cite{meyer2014perceptions}; their
observational follow-up showed that in practice activity switches arrive every
few minutes and work is spread thinly across many tools and artefacts
\cite{meyer2017worklife}. Mark et al.\ showed that interruption is not free even
when work is completed: interrupted tasks were finished faster, but at the cost
of measurably higher stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort
\cite{mark2008cost}.

This literature motivates our apparatus --- a workspace where board, chat, and
code review sit together --- but we are careful about what it licenses. It
explains why an agent that can reach across these surfaces might help; it does
not license any claim that an integrated workspace beats separate tools, which
would require a fragmented-tools arm that this design does not have
(\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{Trust, reliance, and the perception--behaviour gap}

The automation literature settled long ago that the interesting quantity is not
trust but its \emph{calibration}. Parasuraman and Riley organised the failure
modes as misuse, disuse, and abuse --- over-reliance on automation, neglect of
automation that would help, and deployment that ignores the operator entirely
\cite{parasuraman1997humans}. Lee and See framed trust as an attitude toward an
agent's help under uncertainty and vulnerability, and made appropriate reliance
--- trust that tracks actual trustworthiness --- the design target
\cite{lee2004trust}. We adopt their definition, and \S\ref{sec:measures} states
which basis of trust our instrument measures.

Recent AI-assisted decision-making work shows how readily that calibration
breaks. Bansal et al.\ found that adding explanations raised people's acceptance
of AI recommendations without improving joint human-AI accuracy, because
acceptance rose for wrong recommendations as well as right ones
\cite{bansal2021whole}. Bu\c{c}inca et al.\ showed that interventions which
successfully reduce overreliance are disliked and least engaged with by the
people who most need them \cite{bucinca2021trust}. In the software setting,
Ziegler et al.\ found that developers' \emph{perceived} productivity with a code
assistant tracked suggestion acceptance rate more closely than any measure of
retained output \cite{ziegler2022productivity} --- perception keying on a visible
signal rather than on a result.

The pattern across these is that something \emph{visible} about an assistant
drives trust independently of whether it drives performance. Agent scope is
precisely such a visible property: an agent granted more of the workspace
conspicuously does more. No prior work varies an assistant's scope and asks
whether trust follows the scope or follows the effectiveness. That is RQ3, and
it is the finding here most likely to transfer beyond this apparatus --- because
the decision it informs, how much reach to grant an assistant, is one every team
deploying one now has to make.

\section{Study Design}\label{sec:design}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Research questions and hypotheses}

The study manipulates one variable: how much of the workspace an in-app AI
agent can see and act on. All questions stay within what that manipulation
supports.

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1 (primary)] \textbf{Agent scope $\rightarrow$ effectiveness.} Does
    widening the agent's scope over the workspace (chat $\rightarrow$ +board
    $\rightarrow$ +GitHub) make the user more effective, and on which tasks?
  \item[RQ2] \textbf{Reliance.} How much do users delegate to versus verify
    and override the agent, and does that shift with its scope?
  \item[RQ3] \textbf{Trust versus reality.} Does users' trust in the agent
    track its measured effectiveness, or diverge from it?
\end{description}

\noindent Hypotheses:

\begin{description}
  \item[H1 (RQ1)] Wider scope yields shorter baseline-adjusted agent-task times
    \emph{across conditions}. Deliberately not stated as an absolute gain over
    the manual baseline: phase order is fixed, so an agent$-$baseline delta also
    contains ordinary practice (\S\ref{sec:timing}).
  \item[H2 (RQ1)] The gain is \emph{task-dependent} --- minimal on comprehension
    and single-surface tasks, substantial on cross-surface tasks spanning chat
    $\rightarrow$ board $\rightarrow$ GitHub.
  \item[H3 (RQ1)] Wider scope reduces self-reported cognitive load relative to
    baseline more than narrow scope does.
  \item[H4 (RQ2)] Delegation rises with scope, but reliance is imperfectly
    calibrated: users do not verify or override precisely where the agent is
    actually wrong.
  \item[H5 (RQ3)] Trust and perceived speedup need not track measured
    effectiveness --- users may trust an agent that \emph{does more} even when
    it is not faster, or is occasionally wrong.
\end{description}

\subsection{Design}

The design is hybrid: a within-subjects manual baseline followed by a
between-subjects agent condition.

Every participant first completes six manual baseline tasks (B1--B6, condition
C0) with no agent available, establishing their own manual speed and cognitive
load on each task type. NASA-TLX is collected at this point, before any agent
exposure. Each participant is then assigned to one agent condition --- by
adaptive minimisation rather than simple randomisation, for the reason given
below --- and completes six agent tasks (T1--T6) in that fixed condition.
Table~\ref{tab:conditions} gives the three conditions.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{The three agent scope conditions (between-subjects).}
\label{tab:conditions}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}lLL@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Condition} & \textbf{The agent can SEE} & \textbf{The agent can DO} \\
\midrule
C1 --- Chat only     & Chat                    & Advise only; the user executes \\
C2 --- Chat + board  & Chat, board             & Advise and act on board and chat \\
C3 --- Full scope    & Chat, board, GitHub     & Act across chat, board, and GitHub \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Why hybrid.} The per-person C0 baseline lets each participant's
agent-task performance be analysed as a delta from their own manual speed,
absorbing individual skill differences that would otherwise sit between the
between-subjects conditions and sap power. Six baseline tasks --- one per agent
task type, four of them cross-surface --- give a type-matched, stable per-person
anchor rather than a single generic warm-up.

\paragraph{Why between-subjects on condition.} Holding the agent's access fixed
per person avoids within-person contamination: once a participant has seen a
full-scope agent solve a task, they cannot un-see how it should go. It also
keeps the session inside a self-serve 55--65 minute budget.

\paragraph{Capability scales with scope, by design.} An agent cannot act on a
board it cannot see, so visibility and capability are intentionally coupled.
This bundling is the phenomenon under study --- whether giving the agent more
access to the shared workspace helps the user --- and we therefore name the
variable \emph{agent scope} rather than \emph{context breadth} throughout. The
interpretive consequence is stated in \S\ref{sec:threats}.

\subsection{Apparatus}

\skel{One paragraph plus a screenshot figure. Describe Weld only as far as the
study needs: the surfaces, one seeded project, the agent panel. Do not turn this
into a tool paper.}

All participants work in \emph{Weld}, a workspace for coordinating and reviewing
software work: a Kanban task board, channel-based team chat, and GitHub
integration in a single application. Weld deliberately covers the coordination
and review layer around code rather than authoring or execution --- there is no
editor and no build --- which bounds what the study generalises to
(\S\ref{sec:threats}). GitHub is not a separate destination in the interface:
issues and pull requests are attached to the board cards they belong to, so a
participant reaches a pull request by opening its card, and reviews it in an
in-app review surface (description, files-changed diffs, and approve, comment,
or request-changes, plus merge). Merging moves the linked card exactly as a
GitHub webhook would in production use. What Weld re-presents is the part of
GitHub the task set needs and not the rest --- there are no inline review
comments, no issue or pull-request conversation threads, no continuous-integration
output, and no code browsing --- a partial view whose consequences for the claim
we take up in \S\ref{sec:threats}.

Every participant receives a private clone of the same fictional seed project,
``PetTracker'': 12 cards across 3 columns, 2 chat channels with 18 seeded
messages, and 3 pull requests, all 3 of which carry diffs. GitHub is mocked
from fixtures inside the application --- no live API, no
real repository, and outbound write controls hidden --- so participation is
fully self-serve and no participant's actions can affect another's environment
or leak into a real repository. Review verdicts and comments therefore exist
only in our event log, which is what makes them scorable
(\S\ref{sec:measures}).

The agent is presented as a panel inside the workspace
(Figure~\ref{fig:apparatus}). It runs on a single
pinned, dated model snapshot (\code{gpt-5.4-2026-03-05} --- matches the
\code{OPENAI\_MODEL} default in \code{config.rs}; confirm the deploy does not
override it via env var before the run starts) at a fixed
temperature of 0.2 with versioned prompts, held constant for the entire study.
A context gate assembles the agent's prompt per invocation under a default-deny
policy keyed to the participant's condition, and the same gate governs which
tools are exposed --- C1 has no tools at all, C2 may write to the board and draft
chat messages for the participant to send, and C3 may additionally link cards to
issues and pull requests. Every
invocation records a snapshot of exactly what the agent received
(\S\ref{sec:manipcheck}).

\paragraph{Weld does not replace GitHub, and does not need to.} A natural
question is whether a developer would still keep GitHub open beside Weld. For the
manipulation the answer does not matter: the interface is identical in all three
conditions, so whatever reason a participant might have to look elsewhere is a
constant across arms and cannot produce a condition effect. Inside the study the
question does not arise in any case --- no task \emph{can} be completed only on
\code{github.com}, GitHub is served from fixtures, outbound writes are refused,
and links out are suppressed, so all twelve tasks are completable without leaving
the application. We do not monitor other browser tabs, so this is a property of
the design rather than something we observe. The same logic fixes what
generalises: the study varies \emph{what the agent can reach}, not \emph{how it
reaches it}. The identical contrast could be built by handing a C3 agent a GitHub
API token or a tool server, and the finding transfers to any assistant that can
be granted or denied that reach, however it is plumbed --- a familiar
GitHub-shaped review surface is an apparatus choice that keeps the manipulation
from being confounded with learning an unfamiliar review tool, and tool
consolidation is not the independent variable. Outside the study the view is
partial in the ways described above, and a developer working a real change would
still open GitHub for what it omits (\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{Task set}

Twelve tasks --- six manual baselines then six agent tasks --- of roughly 4--5
minutes each. Task content is held constant across conditions; only the agent's
scope differs.

The agent tasks span three use-case types so we can locate \emph{where} scope
matters. Comprehension and single-surface tasks act as controls: they
demonstrate that wider scope does not \emph{universally} help, which is what
makes the cross-surface result credible rather than a blanket ``more is
better.'' Table~\ref{tab:tasks} summarises them; full scripts are in the
replication package.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{The six agent tasks and what each is designed to test.}
\label{tab:tasks}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}llL@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Task} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{What it tests} \\
\midrule
T1 ``What needs doing?''      & Comprehension  & Chat-only should be competitive \\
T2 ``Catch me up''            & Comprehension  & Do richer summaries reduce follow-up work? \\
T3 Draft a status message     & Single-surface (chat)  & Control: extra scope should not help \\
T4 Set priorities on the board& Single-surface (board) & Advising (C1) vs.\ acting (C2/C3) \\
T5 Bug from chat to the board & Cross-surface  & C2/C3 can act, C1 cannot --- core H1/H2 \\
T6 Review and link the PR, tell the team & Cross-surface & Only C3 can reach the PR to identify and link it; the review itself is always the participant's \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Each baseline task matches an agent task type --- B1 single-surface, B6
comprehension, B2--B5 cross-surface --- while using disjoint content, so it
anchors normalisation without teaching the agent tasks' answers.

T6 is constructed so the correct pull request is identifiable \emph{only} with
GitHub scope: chat refers to it vaguely, and the PR's title and body
disambiguate it. It is therefore the single cleanest test of the top of the
ladder --- if C3 does not beat C2 on T6, the GitHub scope is not pulling its
weight, and the data will say so. T6 also carries the most work: the
participant must identify the pull request, read its diff and record a review
verdict, connect it to the right card, and tell the team in chat. Its matched
baseline B4 requires the same review step on a different, unrelated pull
request, so the review cost is anchored per person rather than confounded with
the agent condition.

No condition completes T6 end to end on the participant's behalf. The agent's
tools are card creation, card movement, card editing, drafting a chat message,
and linking a card to an issue or pull request; submitting a review verdict and
merging are not among them, in any condition. Reading the diff and deciding the
verdict is therefore always the participant's work, and what widens with scope
is only the agent's ability to find the right pull request and connect it. This
is deliberate --- an agent that reviews on the user's behalf would make T6 a
test of automation acceptance rather than of scope --- but it caps how much of
T6's cost the top rung can remove, alongside the fixture-sized diffs
(\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\paragraph{The agent drafts chat messages; it never posts them.} The chat tool
produces a draft that the participant reviews, edits if they wish, and sends
themselves. This is a deliberate asymmetry with the board tools, which do write
directly: posting in a teammate's name is the one action here with a social cost
that cannot be undone by a click, and an agent that speaks for the user without
review is a different intervention from the one we set out to study. It has two
consequences the reader should carry. Substantively, every chat outcome in every
condition passes through a human edit-and-send step, so the chat surface is where
scope can help least by construction --- which is worth remembering when reading
T3, the chat-only single-surface task. Methodologically, a draft is an agent
contribution but not an agent-completed action: the participant's send is
recorded as an ordinary user action, so drafts are excluded from the numerator of
the delegation ratio and reported separately (\S\ref{sec:measures}).

Task wording is load-bearing and was fixed in advance. Agent tasks state a goal
and never a route, and state that the assistant \emph{is available} rather than
instructing its use: naming the surfaces would let participants navigate without
the agent, collapsing the manipulation into UI mechanics, and instructing use
would turn RQ2's delegation measure into a compliance measure. T6 reads, in
full: \emph{``The vaccination reminders work isn't connected to its code yet,
and it hasn't been reviewed. Sort that out and let the team know in
\#pettracker. The assistant is available if you want it.''} Baseline tasks are
by contrast deliberately explicit about the route --- they are a per-person
normaliser, equally explicit for every condition, and their purpose is a stable
anchor rather than a test of navigation.

\subsection{Participants and procedure}

\skel{Fill the realised numbers from \code{study\_participants} plus the
demographics block; keep the planned targets as stated in the archived analysis
plan and report deviations honestly.}

We target 20--30 participants (roughly 7--10 per condition), each contributing
six baseline and six agent observations. Eligibility: adults 18 or over, at
least one year of programming experience, and basic familiarity with issue
trackers, team chat, and GitHub. Recruitment is by self-serve link through
course lists, departmental channels, and developer communities; recruitment is
student-focused but not student-limited.

Sessions are unproctored and self-serve. The flow is: consent and eligibility

\paragraph{Baseline-normalised time.} The headline derived measure is each agent
task's active time relative to that participant's own type-matched baseline:
comprehension tasks (T1, T2) against B6, single-surface tasks (T3, T4) against
B1, and cross-surface tasks (T5, T6) against the mean of B2--B5. Every agent
task type therefore has a like-for-like anchor, and the cross-surface anchor ---
where the hypotheses live --- averages four baselines and is the most stable. In
the models the baseline enters as a covariate rather than as a pre-computed
ratio (\S\ref{sec:models}).

\paragraph{Delegation, and what counts as an agent action.} The delegation ratio
is the share of surface actions during the agent phase that the agent itself
carried out: executed tool calls over executed tool calls plus participant
actions. Two definitional choices are worth stating, because a reader cannot
recover them from the name. First, a drafted chat message is not an agent action.
The agent never posts (\S\ref{sec:design}); the participant sends, and that send
is already counted on the user side, so counting the draft as well would place a
single outcome on both sides of the ratio and inflate delegation exactly where
the agent did the least. Drafts are counted and reported separately, so the
drafting channel remains visible. Second, C1 has no tools, so its delegation
ratio is $0$ by construction rather than by participant choice; the measure that
carries C1's reliance signal is whether participants invoked the agent at all and
acted on its advice, not the ratio.

\paragraph{Scoring the TLX.} We report the raw (unweighted) NASA-TLX, the mean of
the six subscales. \code{tlx\_performance} is the one item whose scale runs
against the workload direction --- it is presented anchored Failure to Perfect,
so a high rating means good performance --- and it is reverse-scored before
entering the composite or any subscale delta, in both administrations. The rule
was fixed in the archived analysis plan rather than chosen after seeing the data,
which matters here: the same item is collected twice and the delta is the H3
measure, so a sign error would propagate silently into every load result.

\paragraph{What the view-switch rate does and does not cover.} Because GitHub is
reached from the card it belongs to rather than from a separate destination, a
route-level view change fires for board, chat, and settings navigation but never
for opening a pull request. The view-switch rate is therefore a board--chat
context-switching measure, and traversal into GitHub is captured by the
cross-reference events and \code{github\_view\_opened} instead. The two are
reported together rather than summed, and the perceived-switching survey item is
paired with the board--chat rate only. \todo{Confirm this is still true of the
build used in the run --- it is a consequence of a UI decision, not a study
setting, so a later interface change silently changes the measure.}

\paragraph{Correctness.} Speed without correctness is not effectiveness, so
correctness is scored for every task on a 0--3 rubric and always reported beside
time. Scoring procedure is fixed in advance because it is the one primary
measure produced by judgement rather than instrumentation: two coders score all
tasks from a final-state export with condition, agent transcript, and timing
stripped --- blinding is mandatory, since knowing a participant was in C3 while
judging their output is a direct route to confirming H1. We report
quadratic-weighted Cohen's $\kappa$ \emph{before} adjudication, then resolve
disagreements to consensus, still blind. The rubric is piloted and frozen before
any real session is scored.

\paragraph{Trust, and what we mean by it.} We adopt Lee and See's definition:
trust is \emph{the attitude that an agent will help achieve an individual's
goals in a situation characterised by uncertainty and vulnerability}
\cite{lee2004trust}. Two things follow. First, trust is an \emph{attitude},
distinct both from the behaviour it motivates (reliance) and from the agent's
actual trustworthiness --- and RQ3 is precisely the question of whether those
come apart. Second, an attitude has bases, and we measure one of them. Lee and
See distinguish trust grounded in an agent's \emph{performance} (does it do the
job), its \emph{process} (does it work in a way that makes sense), and its
\emph{purpose} (was it built for ends I share). Our manipulation varies what the
agent can reach, which bears on performance; the survey budget is about two
minutes; so the composite covers the performance basis alone, and claims about
process- or purpose-based trust are outside what this instrument supports.

The composite is therefore three attitudinal items --- \code{trust\_reliable},
\code{trust\_predictable}, \code{trust\_competent} --- and we report Cronbach's
$\alpha$ for it. If $\alpha < 0.70$ we report the three items individually and
treat every composite-based analysis as descriptive. That rule is fixed in
advance so a weak scale cannot be rescued by dropping whichever item is
inconvenient.

The remaining items are measured and reported, but deliberately kept out of the
composite, each for a different reason. \code{trust\_rely} and
\code{trust\_again} are behavioural intentions --- downstream of trust, and also
of the stakes of the task, which are low here by construction.
\code{trust\_verify} is a self-reported behaviour and the survey counterpart to
the behavioural override rate, so it belongs with H4's calibration analysis
rather than in an attitude score. \code{trust\_speed\_perceived} is perceived
\emph{effectiveness}, the very quantity H5 compares against measured
effectiveness; folding it into the composite would collapse the contrast the
hypothesis is about. \code{trust\_context} is the most consequential exclusion:
it is a perception of the manipulation itself and is expected to rise
C1$\rightarrow$C2$\rightarrow$C3, so including it would build the independent
variable into the outcome and all but guarantee that ``trust'' rose with scope.
We use it instead as a subjective manipulation check alongside the
\code{agent\_context\_sent} snapshot (\S\ref{sec:manipcheck}).

\section{Analysis Plan}\label{sec:analysis}
% =====================================================================

registry; instead the full plan --- models, contrasts, exclusion rules, and the

\subsection{Manipulation check}\label{sec:manipcheck}

Reported first, before any inferential result. From the \code{agent\_context\_sent}
snapshot recorded at every invocation, we verify that 100\% of C1 snapshots
contain no board or GitHub content and 100\% of C2 snapshots contain no GitHub
content. This converts the independent variable from an assumption into
evidence; without it the scoping manipulation rests on trust.

\subsection{Primary models}\label{sec:models}

RQ1/H1/H2 are tested with a linear mixed model on log task time,
\[
\log(\mathit{time}) \sim \mathit{condition} \times \mathit{task\_type}
  + \log(\mathit{baseline}) + (1 \mid \mathit{participant}),
\]
with H2 as the \code{condition}~$\times$~\code{task\_type} interaction: does the
condition effect concentrate on the cross-surface tasks? At this sample size the
full interaction is underpowered, so the confirmatory analysis is a
focused contrast fixed in advance --- C3 versus C1 on cross-surface tasks --- with
the omnibus interaction reported as supporting evidence and everything else
flagged exploratory. We lead with effect sizes and 95\% confidence intervals
rather than $p$-values throughout.

Correctness is modelled in parallel with an ordinal cumulative-link mixed model,
\code{correctness \textasciitilde{} condition * task\_type + (1 | participant)},
and reported beside every timing result.

\paragraph{What the confirmatory contrast estimates.} C3-versus-C1 is a contrast
in \emph{agent scope} as \S\ref{sec:design} defines it --- visibility and
capability bundled --- and H1 is a claim about scope, so the contrast is a valid
confirmatory test of H1. It is not a test of context in isolation and we do not
report it as one. The increment that isolates context is C2-versus-C3: both
conditions act, and they differ only in whether GitHub is in reach. That contrast
is pre-specified as the principal secondary analysis, reported with its
confidence interval whether or not it clears significance, with T6 as its
sharpest single instance. Given the sample we will not read a null there as
evidence that GitHub scope does not matter; an interval spanning both zero and a
substantial effect is the honest description, and we will describe it that way
(\S\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Sensitivity}\label{sec:sensitivity}

A study this size should say what it can and cannot detect rather than leave a
reader to work it out. For the confirmatory contrast --- C3 versus C1 on the two
cross-surface agent tasks, eight participants per cell --- we computed the
minimum detectable effect at $\alpha = 0.05$ two-sided and 80\% power, treating
log task time as having total standard deviation $\sigma$, intraclass correlation
$\rho$ across the repeated tasks within a participant, and a per-person baseline
covariate correlated $r$ with the agent-phase outcome. Across the plausible
ranges $\sigma \in [0.4, 0.6]$, $\rho \in [0.4, 0.6]$ and $r \in [0, 0.7]$, the
minimum detectable difference is $d \approx 1.1$--$1.4$, equivalently a 35--56\%
reduction in task time. Under central assumptions ($\sigma = 0.5$, $\rho = 0.5$,
$r = 0.5$) it is $d = 1.20$, a 45\% reduction. A Monte Carlo check of
20{,}000 replications recovers 80--83\% power at those values, confirming the
analytic figure. These are assumed parameters, not pilot estimates; we re-run the
calculation against the realised $\sigma$ and $\rho$ and report the realised
minimum detectable effect in \S\ref{sec:results}. \todo{Re-run
\code{sensitivity.py} on the realised data and update these figures.}

That is a large effect, and saying so is the point. This design detects only a
substantial scope benefit on cross-surface work; a real but moderate benefit ---
a 20\% reduction, say, which would need roughly 50 participants per cell --- will
not reach significance here and must not be reported as absence. It is why the
analysis leads with effect sizes and intervals rather than $p$-values
(\S\ref{sec:models}), why H4 and H5 are labelled exploratory, and why the
per-person baseline enters as a covariate at all: it is the cheapest variance
reduction available. Adding tasks is not a substitute. Because the
between-participant component dominates, doubling the cross-surface tasks from
two to four moves the detectable effect only from 45\% to 42\%; participants,
not tasks, are the binding constraint.

\subsection{Cognitive load (H3)}

NASA-TLX is collected twice, so load is a within-person phase-to-phase delta
rather than a single end-of-session snapshot. The headline test is
$\Delta\mathrm{TLX} = \mathrm{TLX}_{\text{agent}} - \mathrm{TLX}_{\text{baseline}}$
on the composite and per subscale, compared between conditions (Welch ANOVA or
Kruskal--Wallis given the sample size). A finer-grained secondary analysis models
per-task effort with each participant's baseline effort as the anchor.

\subsection{Reliance (RQ2) and the perception--behaviour gap (RQ3)}

RQ2 is answered descriptively from telemetry: delegation ratio by condition; the
agent funnel from invocation through attempted and executed tool calls to
accepted, edited, or undone outcomes; tool error and rejection rates; and
cross-reference traversal counts. H4's calibration claim is tested by crossing
override behaviour against coded agent-error type --- do users override where the
agent is actually wrong?

RQ3 correlates the three-item trust composite (\S\ref{sec:measures}) and
\code{trust\_speed\_perceived} (Spearman) against measured normalised time and
correctness, and characterises any divergence. Each participant contributes one point --- their exit-survey trust
value against a summary of their own measured performance --- pooled across
conditions for the headline correlation, and repeated within each condition as a
secondary that is immune to the seeing-versus-doing confound but thinly powered
(\S\ref{sec:threats}). \code{trust\_rely} crossed with \code{trust\_verify} gives reliance
calibration; perceived context-switching is paired with the behavioural
view-switch rate. Four open-ended items are thematically coded by two coders with
$\kappa$ reported on a shared subset.

\subsection{Timing validity}\label{sec:timing}

Task time is \emph{active engaged time}: the client clock runs only while the tab
is visible, pauses on background or close, resumes on return, and persists across
reloads, so away-time is excluded by construction rather than after the fact.
Each task additionally logs away time, pause count, and an interruption flag. We
analyse active time as primary, run a sensitivity analysis excluding interrupted
tasks, and report the interruption rate.

Because the clock catches stepping away from the tab but not stepping away from a
tab left in front, one further rule was pre-specified: within a task, 180 seconds
or more of visible time containing zero telemetry events is treated as idle and
subtracted. The threshold sits well above a natural pause for reading a channel
or a diff --- tasks run 4--5 minutes and emit events throughout --- and well
below a genuine walk-away. We report how many tasks trigger it and fit the
primary model with and without the correction.

Phase order is fixed: the six manual baselines always precede the six agent
tasks, since agent exposure cannot be undone. An agent$-$baseline delta therefore
contains ordinary practice as well as agent help. Between-condition inferences
are unaffected --- every arm has the identical ordering --- but we never claim
the agent beat manual work.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}
% =====================================================================

\skel{Write these honestly and early --- several are known now and are stronger
stated up front than extracted by a reviewer.}

\paragraph{Scope confounds seeing with doing.} Visibility and capability rise
together by construction, so the C3-versus-C1 contrast confounds seeing with
doing: C1 only advises while C3 acts across three surfaces, and the simplest
reading of any gap is that the agent did the clicking. We therefore state H1 as a
claim about scope rather than about context, and report the contrast as a
confirmatory test of that claim (\S\ref{sec:models}) --- not as evidence about
context per se. The
contrast that isolates context is C2 versus C3 --- both act, and differ only in
GitHub scope --- which this sample can estimate but not decisively test; T6
sharpens it. RQ3 is less exposed to the confound than RQ1: its headline
correlation is between each participant's own perception and their own measured
performance, pooled across conditions (\S\ref{sec:analysis}), so it does not
rest on a between-condition difference at all. It is not immune, since the
pooled correlation still spans arms that differ in both seeing and doing; we
therefore also report it within each condition, where the confound cannot
operate, with the caveat that 7--10 participants per cell makes those estimates
indicative rather than conclusive.

\paragraph{No fragmented-tools arm.} The study cannot speak to whether an
integrated workspace beats separate tools. Integration motivates the apparatus;
it is not a claim we test.

\paragraph{A partial view of GitHub.} Weld re-presents the parts of GitHub the
task set needs --- issues, pull requests, diffs, review verdicts and comments,
and merges --- and not the rest: inline review comments, conversation threads on
issues and pull requests, continuous-integration output, code browsing and
history, and repository administration. A developer working a real change would
still open GitHub for those. This is inert inside the study, since no task
requires any of them and the interface is held constant across conditions, but it
bounds the claim: the finding concerns an assistant's reach across coordination
and review surfaces, not a claim that this workspace subsumes a code host.

\paragraph{The top rung is small, even though it is real.} Participants do
genuinely review in C3 --- they read a diff, record a verdict and a comment,
link the pull request, and can merge it, with the merge moving the linked card
as a webhook would. But the diffs are seeded fixtures of one to two files, so
review here costs a fraction of what review costs on a real change set. That
caps the detectable C2$\rightarrow$C3 effect --- if reviewing is cheap, help
with reviewing is worth little --- and it limits generalisation to real
repositories, where the reading is the expensive part and the effect could
plausibly be larger. The cap is tighter still because the agent cannot review or
merge in any condition (\S\ref{sec:design}): the C2$\rightarrow$C3 gap can come
only from finding and connecting the right pull request, never from the reading. \todo{After the run, report the actual median time spent in
the review surface; if it is very short, say so here rather than letting a
reviewer compute it.}

\paragraph{Coordination, not authoring.} Weld covers the coordination and review
layer around code. Participants never write or run code, so the findings speak to
agents embedded in the tools \emph{around} an editor, not to in-editor coding
assistants, which is where most existing evidence sits.

\paragraph{One model, one moment.} A single pinned snapshot at fixed temperature
buys internal validity at the cost of external validity. The snapshot must also
be capable enough that the top rung is floored by the design rather than by the
model, which is checked against the live model in a pre-run pilot before
recruitment opens. \todo{After the pilot, restate this as what was actually
observed --- it currently promises a check rather than reporting one.}

\paragraph{Fixed phase order.} Practice effects are bundled into any
agent$-$baseline delta (\S\ref{sec:timing}), which is why H1 is stated as a
between-condition difference.

\paragraph{Sample size and setting.} Roughly 7--10 participants per cell, on a
seeded fictional project, in unproctored self-serve sessions, with a
student-weighted sample. \todo{State the realised sample's demographics and be
specific about what it does and does not generalise to.}
